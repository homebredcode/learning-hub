# Voltage division

In a series circuit the current is the same everywhere, but the **pressure drops as you pass each resistor**. The battery's push gets used up along the loop.

![Two resistors in series: 12 V, 4 Ω and 8 Ω, and the voltage left after each one](lessons/electricity/img/voltage-division.svg)

## The worked example

12 V battery, R1 = 4 Ω, R2 = 8 Ω in series.

1. Total resistance: 4 + 8 = **12 Ω**.
2. Current: I = V / R = 12 / 12 = **1 A**, the same through both.
3. Drop across R1: 1 A × 4 Ω = **4 V**.
4. Drop across R2: 1 A × 8 Ω = **8 V**.
5. The drops add back up: 4 + 8 = 12 V. All the pressure is spent by the time you return to the battery.

## The water picture

A pump pushes water through a pipe with two narrow sections. The **flow rate is identical** everywhere, because water has nowhere else to go. But the pressure falls across each narrow section, and the narrower one takes the bigger share.

> Same current everywhere. The bigger the resistor, the bigger its share of the voltage.

## Why it matters

A sensor reading, a dimmer, and a long thin wire to a device are all voltage division. The wire has a little resistance of its own, so it takes a small share. The device gets what is left.

## Check yourself

?? In a series circuit, what is the same everywhere and what changes? || The current is the same everywhere. The voltage changes: it drops across each resistor.

?? R1 = 4 Ω and R2 = 8 Ω in series on 12 V. How much voltage does R2 get? || 8 V. Current is 1 A, and 1 A × 8 Ω = 8 V.

?? Why do the voltage drops always add up to the battery voltage? || The pressure the battery provides is fully used up around the loop. Nothing is left when you return to the start.
