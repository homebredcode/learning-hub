# Motors and the commutator

## Moving charge makes a magnetic field

- Still electrons do nothing magnetic. **Moving** charge creates a magnetic field.
- A wire carrying current has a field wrapping around it in circles. More current, stronger field.
- A magnet has an invisible field too: the region where its force can be felt.

## The push

Put a current-carrying wire inside a magnet's field and the two fields interact. The wire gets **shoved sideways**. That shove is the basis of every electric motor.

To make it strong enough to be useful, a motor winds the wire into a coil with hundreds of turns, and each turn adds to the force.

## The problem: it would stop

A coil that turns half a revolution ends up with its two sides swapped. The side that was pushed up is now on the bottom, and the same current would push it back down. The coil would just rock back and forth.

The fix: **flip the current every half turn**, at exactly the right moment.

## The commutator

It is a split metal ring fixed to the coil's axle. Two fixed contacts, called **brushes**, press against it and feed in the current.

![Split ring with two brushes: before and after half a turn, showing that the ring halves swap which brush they touch](lessons/electricity/img/commutator.svg)

1. Each coil end is connected to one half of the ring (A and B).
2. The brushes don't move. The ring spins under them.
3. After half a turn the halves have swapped places. Coil end A, which touched the + brush, now touches the − brush.
4. So the current through the coil reverses at the right moment, and the push keeps going in the same rotation direction.

> A commutator is a switch that works by spinning. No electronics needed.

## Check yourself

?? Why does a plain coil in a magnet's field not keep spinning? || After half a turn its sides swap places, so the same current pushes it back the way it came.

?? What does the commutator do? || It reverses the current through the coil every half turn, so the push always points the same rotational way.

?? The brushes don't move. How does the current flip anyway? || The ring halves swap places under the brushes as the coil turns, so each coil end switches from the + brush to the − brush.

?? Why do motors use coils with many turns? || Each turn adds to the force, so hundreds of turns give a strong push from a small current.
